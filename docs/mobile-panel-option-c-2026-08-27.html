Ask Nanci on a phoneTwo things had to move: the menu and the panel

On a computer the menu sits down the left and a panel opens beside the conversation. A phone has room for neither. This is where each one went, and what the move costs.

01 · The Sidebar

The menu stops being furniture

On a computer the menu is a slim rail down the left that is always there and widens when you point at it. A phone cannot spare that width, so the rail becomes a drawer: one button in the bar opens it, and the conversation stays visible behind it.

Side by side · the rail is on screen on the left, and nowhere on the right

The rail works on a computer because it costs almost nothing when you are not using it. It sits at 48px showing icons, widens to 256px when the pointer reaches it, and can be pinned open. None of that survives a phone. There is no pointer to hover, and 48px of permanent chrome is a tenth of the screen spent on something the merchant opens a few times a session.

On a phone · the menu closed, and open over the conversation

1It is a drawer, not a rail
There is no width to spare for a permanent rail, so the menu becomes a drawer that slides in over the conversation at 288px, roughly three quarters of the screen, and leaves the rest visible behind a dim.
2One button, always in the same place
It opens from a single button at the left of the brand bar, which never moves and never changes. It is the only control the bar carries: the panel has its own handle, so nothing else needs a button up there.
3No collapsed state to learn
On a computer the rail has two widths and a pin. The drawer has none of that: it is either open or it is not, and it always shows the full menu with labels, so nothing is ever an unlabelled icon.
4It closes the way it opened
Tapping the dimmed conversation closes it, as does the button that opened it. Nothing has to be aimed at precisely.

02 · The Panel

The panel never fully leaves the screen

On a computer a panel opens beside the conversation, up to three at once. A phone has room for one, and it has to sit on top. So when you are finished with it, it does not close: it shrinks to a handle above the message box. Getting it back is a swipe, not a hunt for a button.

The whole point of a panel is that it opens beside what you are doing, so you can use both at once. A phone screen cannot hold two readable things side by side, and everything here follows from that. We built four versions and used them on a real phone. They differ on one question: where does the panel go when you are not looking at it?

Three of them send it away entirely and leave a counter in the top bar as the only sign it ever existed. The fourth keeps it on screen as a handle, and that is the one we kept. It costs a slim strip of the conversation while a panel is open, and it buys back the thing the others lose: you can always see that the panel is there, and reaching it is one swipe from where your thumb already rests.

On a phone · the panel open, and the same panel put away

1
It arrives
Nanci answers, and the panel rises over the conversation. The conversation does not vanish: it settles back slightly and dims, the way one card sits behind another, so it stays clear that it is still there.
2
You work in it
The panel scrolls on its own. The message box below stays live, and if the conversation was offering a next question to ask, that question moves up beside the message box so it can be taken without putting the panel away.
3
You put it away
Drag the card down and it settles into a slim handle above the message box, shedding its background and shadow on the way so only the grip is left. Tapping the dimmed conversation, the back gesture, and the close button all do the same thing.
4
You bring it back
Drag the handle up, or tap it. Both work, so the gesture is never the only way in. If more than one panel is open, a counter on the card moves between them.

What it costs

1No reading two panels at once
Comparing two things side by side is one of the reasons a computer shows three. That is not possible on a phone. You move between them instead, which works for checking something and poorly for comparing.
2The handle takes a little room
Keeping the panel present costs a slim strip of conversation while one is open. This is the deliberate price of the option we chose. The alternatives give that space back, but leave nothing on screen to say a panel exists.
3The handle does not say which panel is waiting
At rest it is a plain grip. With two panels open the counter says there are two, but neither says what they are. Naming the resting panel is the most useful thing we could add next.
4Going deeper looks like going sideways
Opening more detail about what you are reading and opening something new beside it both simply fill the screen, so nobody can tell from the screen which one happened.

The one rule a phone cannot keep

Our panel rules say a panel opens beside what you are doing and never over it, so both surfaces stay usable. A phone cannot honour that literally, and neither can the menu. What we kept instead: whatever opens, the conversation stays visible behind it rather than being replaced, and the message box stays live underneath, so the conversation never stops. Both read as a layer over your work, not a screen that replaced it.

The rule we genuinely lose is subtler. On a computer, opening more detail about what you are reading looks different from opening something new beside it, because you can see where the new panel landed. On a phone both simply fill the screen. We wrote this down as an open question when we set the rules, and it is still open.

Where it stands

Both moves are built and working on a phone today. The panel is not yet what a visitor gets by default, and nobody outside the team has used either one. Two smaller pieces are unfinished: moving between two open panels does not animate, and the layout does not yet leave room for the home indicator bar on newer iPhones. Neither blocks a demo, and both are visible to anyone looking closely.
